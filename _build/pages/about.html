Most of us will spend the last decade of life unwell. That decade, not the length of the life around it, is the problem worth solving.

The global gap between lifespan and healthspan — the years lived in poor health at the end of a life — averaged 9.6 years across 183 WHO member states in 2019, and it is widening. It is wider for women than for men. It is wider in high-income countries than the headline life-expectancy figures suggest. And in India it compounds with an earlier onset of metabolic disease at lower body-mass index than Western reference ranges predict.

Meanwhile a market has grown up around the promise of reversing it. Supplements with mechanism-only data are sold as though they carried outcome trials behind them. Clinics abroad offer cell therapies whose evidence would not survive a journal club. Patients arrive at consultations having already read more marketing than medicine, and they ask their physician a reasonable question that the physician is often given no good way to answer.

What this summit is trying to do

Three things, and not more than three.

  1. Grade the evidence in public. Every claim made from the stage carries its evidence tier on the slide — including the organisers’ claims. A delegate who hears a claim presented above its grade is expected to say so from the floor.
  2. Write something down. A Charter of consensus statements on evidence, consent, advertising, regulation and what a clinician owes a patient when the data is thin. Read, argued, amended and signed on the Sunday by those who choose to be bound by it.
  3. Recognise careful work. The inaugural Longevity 40 honours forty people and organisations chosen by an independent panel. There is no entry fee and the recognition is never purchased.

Who it is for

For the physician asked every week about a supplement, a test or a clinic abroad. For the researcher whose careful trial is drowned out by a louder claim. For the founder building something that should outlast its marketing. And for the patient, who is the reason for all of it.

In practice that means consultants and specialists in internal medicine, endocrinology, cardiology, orthopaedics, dermatology, gynaecology and sports medicine; researchers in cell and molecular biology; allied health professionals and nursing staff; postgraduate trainees; and industry delegates from diagnostics, devices, manufacturing and digital health.

What it is not

It is not a trade show with a lecture programme attached. Sponsors fund the summit and are credited for it, and they do not choose the faculty, the motions or the honourees. Industry sessions are labelled as industry sessions. The Longevity 40 panel sits apart from the organising committee and apart from the sponsors.

It is also not a consensus-by-acclamation exercise. The statements are voted on. Amendments carried are incorporated. A delegate may attend every session and sign nothing.
